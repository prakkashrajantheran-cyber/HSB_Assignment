Understand thalassemia before starting a family

Learn what carrier status means, understand common blood-test terms, and prepare clear questions for your healthcare team before or during pregnancy.

The condition

Thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder; it is not transmissible between people.

Your blood report

MCV describes average red cell size, while MCH describes haemoglobin content. Review all results together with a healthcare professional.

Words on a full blood count

Important: A single blood-test result cannot establish a diagnosis. Interpretation requires consideration of clinical findings, family history and, where appropriate, confirmatory testing.

What is thalassemia?

It is an inherited blood condition that affects how the body makes haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. The type and the effect vary widely, from a silent trait to forms that need a specialist team.

When fewer alpha or beta chains are made, red cells can be smaller, hold less haemoglobin, and sometimes be cleared sooner.

Trait or carrier

Often mild

Many carriers feel well. Blood cells may be smaller than average. A carrier can pass the gene variant to a child.

Intermediate

Needs follow-up

Haemoglobin H disease can cause anaemia that changes over time. Iron is not automatic.

Transfusion-dependent

Specialist care

Beta thalassemia major may need regular transfusions and care to manage extra iron. That is not the same as the trait.

Before planning a pregnancy

  • Ask both partners to discuss thalassemia carrier screening.
  • Thalassemia is genetic and is not contagious.
  • Trait can look like iron deficiency on a basic blood count.
  • Do not give iron supplements unless a doctor has prescribed them.
  • If both partners are carriers of the same relevant globin chain, arrange a genetics or reproductive counselling appointment.

Carrier status often causes few or no symptoms. If both partners carry a relevant variant, each pregnancy has up to a 25% chance of producing a child with severe thalassemia, depending on the condition and inheritance pattern.

Questions for the care team

  • Does this blood result indicate that I am a thalassemia carrier?
  • Is another test needed, such as haemoglobin studies or DNA?
  • Should we test both partners before pregnancy?
  • How could carrier status affect our pregnancy options?
  • Would carrier testing or genetic counselling help our family?
